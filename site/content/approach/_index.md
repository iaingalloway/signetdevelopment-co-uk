Iain Galloway's approach brings decisions about the business and the software into the same delivery process. The people responsible for a service should understand what is changing and have a direct part in deciding when it happens.

## Start with the goal

The first question is what the organisation needs to achieve. That might be removing an obstacle to a buyout, reducing the cost of running a service or ending outages caused by releases.

Technical choices need to serve that goal.

## Put decisions with the people responsible

Different changes need different judgement. An operations manager needs to understand the effect of a release on the service. A security engineer needs to assess changes to access. A technical manager needs to consider capacity and cost.

Their involvement should be part of delivery, not a separate conversation that engineers have to translate into action. Proposed changes are made visible for review, with clear responsibility for approval. The people responsible can then put approved changes into effect through the same process.

## Automate the repetitive work

People should spend their time adding value, not manually assembling context from across systems or performing repetitive tasks.

Sources of truth keep information consistent and reliable across systems. Automated builds, tests, and deploys reduce opportunities for mistakes and keep what happens in the system close to what was reviewed. Records of changes, checks, and approvals make it possible to see what happened and what was intended.

## Check the result and plan for faults

A successful deployment is not the same as a successful change. Monitoring and feedback from the people using the service show whether it is working as intended.

The design also needs to account for failed changes and faults: how the service will keep running, how a problem will be found and how it can be resolved. That work belongs in the delivery plan, not after an outage.

## Leave the team able to run it

The client team needs to understand the system and be able to change it without depending on the people who built it. Documentation, training and practice are part of delivery.

On the logistics project, Iain delivered 120 hours of training to six existing staff so they could operate and maintain the system independently.
